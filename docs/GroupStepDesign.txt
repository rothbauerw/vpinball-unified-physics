GROUP STEP DESIGN: the engine moves balls and parts together (ProjectAssessment-2026-10-27.txt item 0 b)
==========================================================================================================
Started 2026-10-29 with the user, one piece at a time. Replaces the band wiring of UnifiedContactDesign.txt 12.3 (written for the old node band).
Decision (user, 2026-10-29): no part moves its own balls. The engine moves every ball and part of a touching group together, with the band's discrete-gradient
solve (docs/BandCordDesign.txt section 3: forces from exact energy differences, losses on mid-step speeds, drives as counted work). The band cord (src/physics/
bandcord.h, proved offline) is the first part. Gates, spinners, bumpers, the turntable disc and the flipper bats keep today's coupling (force out, reaction a step
later) until each moves over with its own offline proof (to-do 1.3).

1. What a part supplies   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
-----------------------------------------------------------
The part describes itself; the engine does all the moving.
   1. Bodies: what can move (positions, angles) and the mass or inertia of each; inertia may depend on position (the sling plate's I(phi)).
   2. Stored energy at any position (springs, cord stretch, squash), with the EXACT energy difference between two positions, not only a force: that is what keeps
      the books closed at any step.
   3. Stiffness now: a rough value, for the step length and the solve's first guess (the Newton Jacobian).
   4. Losses: forces from mid-step speeds that can only remove energy (damping, friction, rolling resistance, with their limits).
   5. Drives (coils, motors): forces whose work is counted.
   6. Contacts with balls: where a ball touches and how close it is (geometry). The law is the shared rubber / steel law restated as stored energy plus losses
      (to-do 1.20).
   7. Hard stops (the sling's coil stop): the step ends exactly on them; they are inelastic.
   8. An end-of-step update that changes no energy (the band re-routing round a post: the cord length is C1 through it, so no energy jump).
   9. Save and restore of its state, and its events (as today: SaveState / RestoreState, the event list).
The engine: forms the touching groups, picks the step length, solves the group's moves together, runs the shared guards and the energy books.
Fixed surfaces (playfield, walls, posts) supply only 6 and 7, with no bodies. Ball-ball contact is a 6 too. A ball on the band that also rolls on the playfield and
touches another ball is in one solve, with no lag between contacts.
Open (ProjectAssessment item 0, not tested): the cost of the solve per step; whether it settles in a pile of touching balls; the contact's surface curvature held
fixed within a step.

2. How groups form   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
------------------------------------------------------
A group is today's island (contactsystem.cpp FindIslands), with the same rules: two balls share one when they can touch within the cycle (gap below the ball
reach plus twice their closing speed times the cycle), when they are on the same band, or when they touch the same moving part (gate, spinner, bumper, disc, bat).
Fixed surfaces (playfield, walls, posts) link no balls: they are contacts of each ball inside its group. Nothing new to build for the grouping.
Open: a group's solve grows with its ball count (a pile of six is one group of six); whether it settles quickly there is measured offline (piece 1 open list).

3. Which groups use the new step first   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Groups holding a band switch first: the band is proved offline, and its groups are where the energy problem showed. Every contact of a ball in such a group
(playfield, walls, posts, other balls) is in the same solve, so the shared contact law restated as stored energy plus losses (to-do 1.20) is built and used then.
Other groups keep today's stepping until an offline proof on the steel cases (a 3 m/s ball into a wall, a pile of six balls: accuracy, energy, cost); then they
switch with no new code. Groups with a gate, spinner, bumper, disc or bat follow as each part moves over (piece 1 decision).
During the move two steppings exist; a ball's group is decided once per cycle, so a ball changes stepping only between cycles. Each move has a physics-ini switch
for an old / new comparison on the same table, removed once the move is proved.
Open: a band group that also holds a gate, bumper or bat keeps that part's one-step lag inside the group until it moves over (rare: a ball between a sling and a
flipper); reported on screen when it happens (rule 8).

4. The contact's push-back (normal direction) as stored energy plus losses   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Stored energy: the squash law as a closed-form energy at any depth (Hertz + the thin bonded layer at the thickness left; the formula the band uses, BandCordDesign
section 2); post and wall rubbers get the identical law; steel (playfield, metal walls, ball-ball) is plain Hertz, the same formula with no layer.
Losses: every damping term is a force from the mid-step speed, so it only removes energy: the rubber's squash damping (alpha, 0.18), the hard surfaces' calibrated
viscoelastic damping and their flat loss (normal_loss).
A contact found in the middle of a step cannot give back more than went in: the step uses the exact energy difference.
Open: the surface's curvature is held fixed within a step; a ball crossing from one mesh face to the next keeps the energy exact but takes an averaged force
direction for that step (edge_radius_mm rounds the edges). The squash energy needs a logarithm: slower than plain Hertz, measured in the offline proof.

5. Sideways grip   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
----------------------------------------------------
Every contact of the shared law keeps the sideways (shear) spring: post and ring rubbers, rubber walls and primitives, the flipper rubber once the bat moves over,
and steel (playfield, metal walls, ball-ball; there the spring is stiff and the touch short, so the effect is small). Rubber gives sideways about two thirds as
much as inward; a ball gripping a post on a glancing hit winds the spring up and is pushed back as it leaves (spin reversal, the superball effect). Dropping it
would change how balls leave posts with no data to justify it.
Energy: the wind-up is stored energy (its stiffness follows the squash depth, inside the energy, so nothing leaks as the ball leaves); slip past the friction
limit is a loss that can only remove energy. Twist and rolling resistance stay losses.
The band keeps its proven grip (stick or slide, no spring) for now; moving it to the same spring law later is in the to-do, so every rubber ends on one law.
Open: each touching contact carries its wind-up in the saved state and the solve has more to find (cost, measured offline). The offline proof adds oblique post hits
with the spring and with the simple grip, so the difference is measured. The bat's energy gain (to-do 2.10) is suspected to sit in this part of the law: INFERRED
that the new form removes it, checked when the bat moves over.

6. Step length and the guards that stay   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------
Step length by accuracy only (the step cannot make energy at any length): the stiffest body of the group, omega h <= omega_h (0.6 from the band sweep; steel
impacts get their own value from their offline proof); a surface being closed on is reached in about two steps; a ball turns at most 2 deg per step round a band
or post; a step ends exactly on a hard stop and at the end of the 1 ms cycle.
Guards that stay: engagement (closing speed plus the part's speed bound); the sweep against tunnelling through thin walls; the rule for a ball found already
overlapping a surface (the seat); halve-and-retry from the saved state when a step's solve does not settle; the group's energy books closing every cycle, a
failure shown on screen (rule 8).
Guards that go for groups on the new step: the extra rewinds and depth checks that existed only to catch energy being made.
Open: whether 0.6 is accurate enough for steel impacts (decided by the steel proof).

7. Offline proof   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
----------------------------------------------------
The group step alone, no engine (runs/<date>-group-step). Each case at the engine's step choice and at fixed steps 2 us - 1 ms, against a 1 us run.
Cases:
   Steel: a ball dropped on the playfield until it rests; a ball into a metal wall at 1 and 3 m/s, head-on and at 45 deg; two balls head-on at 3 m/s; three
      balls in a row hit from one end (cradle); a pile of six balls against a wall (the solve settles; cost).
   Rubber: head-on post hits at 0.1, 0.32, 0.86, 3 m/s against the rebound targets (to-do 1.21); glancing post hits at 30 and 60 deg, 1 and 3 m/s, with the
      shear spring and with the simple grip; a ball pinched between a wall rubber and a post; a ball resting against a rubber on the slope.
   Band: the 19 band cases (runs/2026-10-29-band-cord) with the playfield and gravity in; a ball touching the band and a post at once; two touching balls on the
      band; a sling hit with a second ball resting against the first.
   Past launch cases: a contact found in the middle of a step; the sling hit while its coil drives.
Pass, each run on its own:
   1. Energy never rises beyond the drives' work (coil, gravity): with losses the per-step rises sum to <= 1 uJ and the books close within 1 uJ; with no losses
      and no coil the energy stays within 1 uJ at every step.
   2. No ball passes through a wall, post or band.
   3. Every step's solve settles.
   4. At the engine's step choice: exit speed within 5 % and direction within 2 deg of the 1 us run.
   5. Cost: at most 30 us per 1 ms of contact for one ball; piles and multi-ball cases reported, not judged.
Stop rule: the whole set once; anything fails -> stop and report; no patching without the user.
Not covered: the engine's loop (grouping, engagement, guards): the engine proof after this one.

8. How it plugs into the engine   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
-------------------------------------------------------------------
1. The band becomes a part object: cord, sling plate, coil and switch blades, settings from the shared rubber keys. It replaces the old node band (hitrubber.cpp
   BandChain); the old band stays behind the physics-ini switch only for an old / new comparison and is deleted once the new one is proved.
2. The cycle is unchanged except for one fork: islands form as today; an island holding a band goes to the group step instead of today's fixed 5 us steps;
   every other island goes where it goes now.
3. The group step, per 1 ms: gathers the island's balls, the band and each ball's contacts (playfield, walls, posts, other balls); steps them together to the
   cycle's end under the guards of piece 6; writes the balls back; fires the events after (band hit, switches, the sling's own fire).
4. Forces from outside the description (magnets, the table nudge, a part not yet moved over such as a gate or bat in the same group) enter as drives: held over
   the step, their work counted in the books.
5. A band with no ball rings down and its plate moves by itself with the same solve and no balls.
6. The drawn band reads the cord's path after the step. Band animation is broken today (to-do 3.9): this gives it the right shape; the animation fix is separate.
Breaks / open: the old node band's own keys (node spacing, band_stops, ...) change meaning or go: each listed in NewPhysicsReference.txt, with an on-screen note
when a table sets one (rule 8). Switch and sling events fire after the step (same cycle, order against other events may change). Tables with unified = 0 keep the
stock bands.

10.1 How the band joins the solve, and the ball's height   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
   One solve. Each ball keeps its 6 unknowns (move and spin); the band adds no ball unknowns; the sling plate's angle is the one added unknown (none for a band
   without a plate). The band reads each ball's centre projected into its plane (the playfield plane at the cord's height: a fixed linear map), and its forces and
   stiffness are mapped back to 3D the same way.
   Height is in the energy (user: "This is a 3D simulation and bands can throw or push a ball into the playfield depending on the height they are placed"): the cord
   stays at its own height between its posts; round a ball the path's clearance is sqrt((R + r)^2 - dz^2) (R ball radius, r cord radius, dz = ball centre height -
   cord height), so the cord's length and stored energy depend on the ball's height, and the push comes out along the 3D line from the cord's centre line to the
   ball's centre: a cord below the centre lifts the ball, above it pushes the ball into the playfield (the playfield contact takes it in the same solve), level gives
   no vertical push. The backstop squash (posts, stops, plate face) is measured along the same 3D line. Still one function of the positions: the exact step and the
   energy books are unchanged; cost one square root more per ball on the band. Replaces BandCordDesign.txt section 6's "planar" note (which gave no vertical push).
   Not handled: the cord slipping up or down along the ball (grip holds it while the contact line's tilt has tan < mu 0.9, about 42 deg or dz up to about 11 mm with
   a 27 mm ball and 5.4 mm cord, CALCULATED; past that a real cord slides, the model keeps it at its height); the cord sliding up or down a post (posts hold it at a
   fixed height). A ball hopping over the band: the contact fades smoothly as dz reaches R + r (16.2 mm), so it can pass over.
10.2 The band's grip in the 3D solve   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
   The band keeps its proven grip (stick or slide, no shear spring; BandCordDesign 2), turned into 3D: the friction acts in the 3D contact plane (along the cord, and
   across it tilted with the ball's height), twist about the 3D contact line, rolling resistance about the axis across the cord in that plane; the limit is the band's
   whole push on the ball; the grip impulses are found between the solve's rounds as in the band's own solve (maximum dissipation: their work is never positive).
   Exception to the one-shared-path rule (to-do): two grip laws in one solve until the band moves to the shear spring, which needs a patch-size rule for a contact
   pressed by tension alone (no squash depth), with its own design and proof; the band is not called done until then.
   Not handled: the give-back of the rubber's sideways bend on a glancing band hit (a post hit has it; 1a measured up to 0.11 m/s and 7 deg between spring and
   near-rigid grip at 1 m/s on posts). Open (INFERRED): a ball gripping the band and the playfield at once may need more rounds (the two grips meet through the
   ball's spin); the smoke run shows it.
10.3 The step length with a band in the group   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
   The band's rules join the group's ChooseStep (the shortest wins): a ball on the cord at omegaH 0.6 on the cord's stiffness and at most 2 deg of turn round it a
   step; a squash on a post, stop or the plate face reached in about two steps and stepped at 0.6 while it is struck; the plate's springs at 0.6, at most 2 deg of
   turn a step, a step ending exactly on the coil stop. The steady rule applies to the band's squash too: touching with its compression rate and slip rate within
   vSteady (0.05 m/s) it takes omegaHRest (2.4), as every steady contact does. The cord itself is soft and never sets short steps.
   Not handled: a ball resting on the band that is struck, or resting on the sling as it fires, keeps the relaxed step until its rate passes vSteady (one step late;
   energy safe, that step less accurate), as for every steady contact.

11. The cord free to move in height (replaces 10.1's cord held at its height between the posts; designed with the user one question at a time, 2026-10-29)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Why (10.4 traces): the fixed-height cord turns a small lift into a run-away throw, has a singular push when a ball's centre comes over it, and leaves its back span
on a lifted ball (an unbooked energy drop when it lets go).
11.1 What sets the cord's height   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
   The cord takes the shortest path in 3D. Only its own posts hold its height (they are shaped to the rubber: it does not slip up or down them, user). Between them
   it moves up and down freely; it stays massless (no new unknowns). A ball is a sphere the cord's centre line keeps R + r from: a ball above the cord's height
   pushes it down, below lifts it, and the pull on the ball is along the real 3D line; a ball centred on the cord gets no vertical push; one resting on top presses
   it down. Stop posts and the plate face: the cord slides up and down on them (alone they keep it flat: its ends are held at one height). The playfield is a floor:
   the cord's centre stays at least r above it. The cord's length stays a smooth function of the positions (the exact step holds); the back span sees the ball as
   a sphere too and lets go of a lifted ball smoothly.
   Not handled: the cord's weight and sag; the path's shape assumes the cord slides freely over the ball (the grip still acts on the ball, as in-plane today);
   cost (one ball in a span: closed form; two or more: repeated passes). The roll join / leave fault and the lossless take-off loss (10.4) stay open.
11.2 How the 3D path is found, and what the cord can touch   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
   The shortest path is solved exactly (to rounding) at every evaluation, by Newton's method on where the cord meets and leaves each pulley, started from the path
   of the step's start: on an own post a point on its groove (fixed height); on a ball two points on its sphere joined by the shortest arc; on any other surface a
   point the cord's centre line keeps r off it. Exact because the energy check needs the length to be a smooth function of the positions. The pull on a ball is
   the tension times the difference of the two runs' directions (a property of the shortest path; the force check confirms it). A pulley joins where a straight
   run cuts into it and leaves where its wrap goes below zero, both with the same 3D geometry (the roll fault, 10.4, is fixed here).
   What the cord touches (user: "it shouldn't matter what it hits, wall, post, playfield, or rail, it should follow the same squash law as a post"): its own posts
   (hold its height and place); every other surface (posts, walls and rails behind it, the plate's face, the playfield) is one kind: the cord lies against it and
   slides on it, and a ball pressing the cord onto it squashes the cord with the post law. No surface is a special case; the offline build sets them by hand, the
   engine's finding them from the table (to-do 3.7) is decided at the wiring. A real band never reaches the playfield (user); it is in the list for the one rule.
   Cost (INFERRED, to be timed): one ball between two posts 2 - 4 Newton passes on a few unknowns; six balls a small linear solve a pass.
   Not handled: a path solve that does not settle halves the step (the solver's rule); a wrap may not change by more than half a turn in a step (as now); the cord's
   friction on the surfaces it slides on is left out (as on the posts).
11.3 The band's grip with the cord free in height   (approved by the user 2026-10-29; replaces 10.2's across-the-cord friction against a still cord)
   Across the cord: while the grip holds, the cord's touch on the ball stays on the same spot of the ball (it turns with the ball's spin about the cord's direction):
   a rolling ball drags the cord down or up with its surface, the cord leaves its shortest path and its tension pulls back through the path (the cord's own stiffness,
   like a plucked string). Past mu x the push the touch slides back toward the shortest path; that sliding is the friction's loss. Energy: while held the length is a
   function of the positions and the held spot (the exact step holds); sliding only removes energy. Along the cord, twist and rolling resistance: as 10.2. This is
   the shear-spring grip of the other rubbers with the cord's tension as the spring (most of the way to one shared law).
   Not handled: each touching ball carries its held spot in the saved state (more state and solving: cost measured); the rubber's own sideways give (shear spring)
   still left out; friction along the cord ignores the cord sliding round its posts.

11.10 The cord's friction on the surfaces it is pressed onto   (approved by the user 2026-10-29)
   Where a ball squashes the cord onto a surface (the plate face, a stop post, a wall, an own post), that surface grips the cord too: the same stick / slide law as
   the ball's grip, limit mu x the squash force there. It holds the cord's position up and down on the surface (along its length the cord still slides freely: one
   tension round the loop). The squashed cord is held by both: while both hold, the ball's spin cannot drag it; the grip that reaches its limit first slides (a
   rolling ball against a still plate: the ball slides on the cord and its spin is spent on friction instead of winding the cord round it). Each friction only
   removes energy (Coulomb). Before the squash starts nothing changes. mu: the band's (rubber on steel) for the plate and posts; a wall its own material's.
   Not handled: the cord sliding along its length on the surfaces; the switch blades' force (immaterial, user); the cord's twist and mass; cost (one more stick /
   slide test per pressed ball per step: measured). Proof: as 11.5 (lift limit, no ball past the band) on the sling at 70 % (1, 2.5, 3 m/s, and a hit between the
   plate and a switch) and Rubber3: the cord should stop near the ball's mid point and the ball not launch.
